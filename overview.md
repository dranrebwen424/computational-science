RESEARCH OVERVIEW
The study aims to:
1. Determine the level of English language anxiety among Grade 12 students,
measured across four dimensions:
• Communication Anxiety
• Fear of Negative Evaluation
• Test Anxiety
• English Class Anxiety
2. Assess the degree of existence of potential predictors of language anxiety:
• Self-confidence
• Language Proficiency
• Peer Influence
• Classroom Environment
3. Identify the major predictors of language anxiety in oral communication.
4. Propose evidence-based interventions to address language anxiety in the
institution.

TYPE OF DATA COLLECTED
• Primary data from surveys administered to Grade 12 students, including:
o Responses to anxiety-related items (rated on a Likert scale)
o Ratings of predictor variables (self-confidence, proficiency, etc.)
o Demographic information (age, sex, academic strand)
• Secondary data from statistical analysis of survey results (descriptive and
correlational metrics).
DATA FORMAT
• Quantitative: Structured numerical data including means, frequencies,
percentages, and Pearson correlation coefficients.
• Organized As: Spreadsheets (CSV/Excel) with labeled variables, and summary

tables of statistical results.
DESCRIPTION OF THE DATASET
The dataset is quantitative and structured, derived from surveys of 237 Grade 12
students at Mabini Colleges, Inc. Key components include:
• Anxiety Variables: 4 dimensions (Communication Anxiety, Fear of Negative
Evaluation, Test Anxiety, English Class Anxiety) with mean scores for each.
• Predictor Variables: 4 factors (Self-confidence, Language Proficiency, Peer
Influence, Classroom Environment) with rated levels.
• Demographic Variables: Age, sex, and academic strand (e.g., STEM, ABM,
HUMSS).
• Statistical Data: Pearson correlation coefficients linking predictors to anxiety
dimensions, and frequency distributions for subgroup comparisons.

PROPOSED VISUALIZATION TECHNIQUES
1. Interactive Bar Charts:
• Compare mean levels of each anxiety dimension and predictor across
demographic subgroups (e.g., strands, sex).
2. Heatmap:
• Display correlation strength between predictors and anxiety dimensions
(color-coded for clarity).
3. Scatter Plots with Filters:
• Show relationships between individual predictors (e.g., Self-confidence)
and anxiety levels, with options to filter by strand or sex.
4. Interactive Dashboard:
• Integrate all visualizations into a single interface, allowing users to toggle
between variables, zoom into data points, and view summary statistics on
hover.
5. Conceptual Framework Diagram:
• Interactive network map showing how predictors relate to anxiety and the
study’s theoretical foundations.
JUSTIFICATION OF INTERACTIVE FEATURES
1. Subgroup Filtering:
• Enables exploration of how anxiety and predictors vary by demographic
factors (e.g., do STEM students have higher test anxiety?).
2. Hover-Over Statistics:
• Provides immediate access to exact means, correlations, or frequencies
without cluttering the main visual.
3. Toggle Between Variables:
• Allows users to easily compare relationships across different anxiety
dimensions and predictors.
4. Zoom Functionality:
• Helps focus on specific data points (e.g., strong correlations) for detailed
analysis.
SCIENTIFIC REASONING BEHIND CHOSEN TYPES
• Bar Charts: Ideal for comparing categorical data (e.g., anxiety levels across
strands) – aligns with the study’s descriptive objectives.
• Heatmap: Effectively communicates correlation strength, which is critical for
identifying major predictors (core to the study’s correlational design).
• Scatter Plots: Show trends and variability in relationships between continuous
variables (e.g., Language Proficiency vs. Communication Anxiety).
• Dashboard: Ensures the visualization is user-friendly and supports systematic
exploration of findings, consistent with scientific inquiry principles.
EXPECTED ANALYTICAL INSIGHTS
• Clear identification of which predictors (e.g., Self-confidence) have the strongest
link to specific anxiety dimensions (e.g., Fear of Negative Evaluation).
• Visual confirmation of whether anxiety levels vary significantly by academic
strand or sex.
• Enhanced understanding of how the study’s theoretical framework applies to
real-world data.
• Support for the proposed interventions by highlighting key areas for improvement
(e.g., classroom environment adjustments if linked to high anxiety).
