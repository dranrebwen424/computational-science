Chapter 1 

THE PROBLEM AND ITS BACKGROUND 

Background of the Study 

Language is considered the primary goal of education. English is said to be the most spoken language. Activities such as class discussions, oral presentations, and recitations often require students to speak in the English language. However, many students experience fear, nervousness, or discomfort when asked to speak in public using the language. This feeling is commonly referred to as language anxiety, particularly in oral communication. Aside from cultural value, the economic value of English is remarkably high in India, as even a layman uses this language in his or her ’native’ communication. It is noticed that even English illiterates give importance to English and want their children to learn English for their bright future. 

When applied to language learning, language anxiety refers to the fear or apprehension experienced when a person is required to use a second language in communication, particularly in speaking. This speaking anxiety, often driven by fear of negative evaluation from peers and teachers, is a major predictor of oral communication anxiety (Weda & Sakti, 2018). This fear causes students to feel nervous and hesitant, especially when responding to questions or speaking in front of native speakers. Students reported that their lack of familiarity with English expressions and fear of committing grammatical mistakes made them hesitant to participate in oral activities. This highlights the direct relationship between language proficiency and communication anxiety (Ramos, 2019). Furthermore, many Grade 12 students in public schools feel unprepared for oral communication tasks due to their perceived inability to express themselves fluently. This perceived inadequacy often results in anxiety when they are required to speak in front of their peers or teachers (Bautista and Villarino, 2020). These anxieties can significantly impede language learning and negatively impact students’ academic performance and overall well-being. 

There are many possible predictors of language anxiety. These include personal factors such as self-confidence, fear of negative evaluation, language proficiency, and past learning experiences. In addition, environmental factors such as the classroom atmosphere, teacher support, peer interactions, and the nature of speaking tasks can significantly influence a student’s level of anxiety.  

Language anxiety can significantly affect a student’s self-confidence and academic performance. In fact, in Saudi Arabia, it is the most studied foreign language, and many English language courses are offered from primary to advanced levels at many schools, institutions, and universities. 

Although numerous studies have been conducted on language anxiety, most research focuses broadly on foreign language anxiety without isolating oral communication, which is often the most anxiety-inducing skill. For example, Sansaluna, Bawa, Camua, and Untong (2021) found that Grade 12 students in Maguindanao experienced high levels of speaking anxiety linked to grammar, phonology, and meaning systems. However, their study did not explore other social and psychological predictors, such as self-confidence or peer influence, which may equally contribute to anxiety. Similarly, Buscabus and San Jose (2025) revealed that English language anxiety did not significantly mediate between classroom environment and strategy use among senior high school students, leaving a gap in identifying which specific predictors directly influence oral communication anxiety. Furthermore, there is limited recent research on private institutions like Mabini Colleges Inc., where classroom dynamics and learning resources may differ from public schools, suggesting the need for more context-specific studies.   

This study is significant because it directly addresses the gaps identified in recent literature. By focusing specifically on oral communication anxiety among Grade 12 students at Mabini Colleges Inc., the research aims to capture the most pressing dimension of language anxiety in the classroom. Unlike previous studies that examine only isolated factors, this study considers multiple predictors—such as self-confidence, language proficiency, peer influence, and classroom environment—to determine which factors, have the strongest relationship with anxiety. Doing so provides a more comprehensive understanding of the phenomenon. The findings will not only extend existing knowledge but also offer practical insights for teachers and administrators in designing interventions to build students’ confidence, reduce fear of negative evaluation, and improve classroom engagement. As highlighted by Buscabus and San Jose (2025), further exploration of anxiety predictors is necessary to fully understand its impact on learning outcomes. 

This study aims to investigate the predictors of language anxiety in oral communication among Grade 12 students of Mabini Colleges Inc. Understanding these predictors will not only provide insight into the challenges students face but will also help educators develop strategies and interventions that create more supportive and encouraging learning environments. The ultimate goal is to reduce anxiety and enhance students’ confidence and competence in using English for oral communication.

The study aims to determine the predictors of language anxiety in oral communication among Grade 12 students of Mabini Colleges Inc.

This study will focus on the predictors of language anxiety in oral communication among Grade 12 students of Mabini Colleges Inc. The study will be limited to determining the (a) level of English language anxiety in terms of communication apprehension, fear of negative evaluation, test anxiety, and English class anxiety; (b) degree of existence of the predictors such as self-confidence, language proficiency, peer influence, and classroom environment; (c) the major predictors of language anxiety; and (d) proposed interventions to address the issue.

The findings of this study will be limited to the context of Mabini Colleges Inc. and may not be generalized to other institutions without further validation.

Theoretical Framework 

           This study is anchored on several interrelated theories that elucidate the factors influencing language anxiety in oral communication: 

Anxiety/Uncertainty Management (AUM) Theory 

          Developed by William B. Gudykunst, AUM theory posits that effective communication, especially in intercultural contexts, hinges on managing both anxiety and uncertainty. In the realm of second language acquisition, learners often experience heightened anxiety due to unfamiliar linguistic and cultural norms. AUM suggests that when individuals manage their anxiety within optimal thresholds, communication effectiveness improves, thereby reducing language anxiety in oral interactions. 

Broaden-and-Build Theory of Positive Emotions 

          Proposed by Barbara Fredrickson, this theory asserts that positive emotions expand individuals’ thought-action repertoires, fostering resilience and building personal resources. In the context of language learning, positive emotions like enjoyment can enhance learners’ motivation and confidence, mitigating the adverse effects of language anxiety. Conversely, negative emotions may narrow focus and impede language acquisition. 

Willingness to Communicate (WTC) Model  

          The WTC model suggests that a learner's readiness to engage in communication is influenced by various factors, including motivation, confidence, and anxiety. High levels of language anxiety can negatively impact a student's willingness to communicate, thereby affecting oral proficiency.

Definition of Terms 

 

 

Definition of Terms 

 

The following terms were defined conceptually or operationally for clearer understanding of this research study. 

English Language Anxiety. also known as foreign language anxiety, is a psychological state characterized by fear or tension experienced during the learning or use of English. It refers to the feeling of worry, nervousness, or unease experienced by students when using English in oral communication, assessed through the following dimensions: 

           Communication Apprehension. The fear or anxiety associated with actual or anticipated communication with others using the English language. It is a degree or measure of the anxiety triggered by the real or anticipated communication act 

          Fear of Negative Evaluation. refers to the fear individuals actively hold about possible negative evaluations given by others in social situations. The apprehension about being judged unfavorably by others while speaking English. 

          Test Anxiety. It is a psychological condition in which people experience extreme distress and anxiety in testing situations. The tension and worry are specifically related to assessments and speaking tests conducted in English. 

          English Class Anxiety. refers to feelings of unease, worry, or nervousness experienced in classroom settings, particularly in situations that require active participation. The general feeling of discomfort or nervousness occurs during English classes that require oral participation. 

           Self-confidence. Refers to the faith in one’s abilities & judgments, crucial for taking positive risks & pursuing goals. The belief in one’s ability to successfully communicate using the English language. 

          Language Proficiency. refers to someone's ability to speak a language. There are levels to proficiency and speaking a second or third language proficiently may increase your prospects as a job candidate. The level of competence and skill of the respondent in using English, particularly in speaking. 

          Peer Influence. It is a nebulous construct in cognitive terms, but it is one that has been studied with particular interest with regard to adolescent social cognition and behavior. The impact that classmates and schoolmates have on the respondent’s comfort and performance in English oral communication.

RESEARCH METHODOLOGY 

This chapter presents the methods of research, population of the study, sample size and sampling techniques, description of the respondents, research instrument, validation of instrument, data gathering procedure, and statistical treatment of data used in the conduct of the study. 

Method of Research 

The study will utilize the quantitative method of research. Based on the conceptual framework and in consideration of the objectives, the researcher will use the descriptive-correlational method. 

Using descriptive statistics, this research will identify the level of English language anxiety in terms of communication apprehension, fear of negative evaluation, test anxiety, and English class anxiety, as well as the degree of existence of the predictors such as self-confidence, language proficiency, peer influence, and classroom environment. 

Using the correlational method, the significant relationship between the profile of the respondents and the degree of existence of the predictors of language anxiety will be determined, as stated in the fourth specific problem of the study.       

Through this, the study aims to find out if and how the students’ demographic profiles are related to the factors influencing their language anxiety in oral communication. 

 

Population, Sample Size and Sampling Technique 

             The Grade 12 students of Mabini Colleges Inc. were the respondents of this study. 

             The researcher will use Cochran formula to compute the sample size. There are 1,254 Grade 12 students enrolled at Mabini Colleges Inc. for the school year 2024–2025. To ensure fair representation across different strands, the Stratified Random Sampling method will be used. In this method, students will be grouped based on their academic strand (STEM, HUMSS, ABM, TVL, GAS), and 

respondents will be randomly selected proportionally from each group. 

Description of the Respondents 

There were 237 respondents composed of Grade 12 students enrolled in different academic strands at Mabini Colleges Inc. The respondents included male and female students aged 17 to 20 years old, coming from STEM, ABM, HUMSS, TVL, and GAS strands. This diverse group of respondents provided a balanced representation of the senior high school population in the institution. 

Research Instrument 

             The researcher designed a survey questionnaire as the principal tool used in gathering the information needed in this study. The questionnaire for the students was divided into two parts: 

             The first part provided for the assessment of the students’ level of English language anxiety in terms of communication apprehension, fear of negative evaluation, test anxiety, and English class anxiety. The second part determined the degree of existence of the predictors of language anxiety, namely self-confidence, language proficiency, peer influence, and classroom environment.

Proposed Intervention to Address the Language Anxiety in Oral Communication 

 

Based on the results of this study, the researchers proposed a set of targeted interventions to address the identified levels and predictors of English language anxiety in oral communication among Grade 12 students of Mabini Colleges Inc. These interventions aim to reduce students’ anxiety, enhance their confidence, and promote a more supportive and learner-centered English classroom environment: 

Considering that most indicators of communication anxiety were consistently experienced by the students, the researchers propose the implementation of Progressive Oral Communication Activities. These activities may include guided pair and small-group discussions, role-playing, impromptu speaking with adequate preparation time, and low-stakes speaking tasks that gradually increase in difficulty. Such strategies are intended to lessen fear, nervousness, and discomfort when speaking in English by allowing students to practice in less threatening and more supportive contexts. In addition, reducing excessive external pressure during oral assessments is recommended. Teachers may place greater emphasis on formative feedback rather than strict grading to encourage participation and minimize students’ reluctance to speak. Creating a positive classroom atmosphere where mistakes are treated as a natural part of the learning process may further increase student engagement and improve learning outcomes.  

To address students’ fear of negative evaluation, particularly embarrassment over making mistakes, the researchers propose the use of Error-tolerant and Supportive Feedback Strategies. Teachers may provide constructive feedback that focuses on improvement rather than highlighting errors alone. Establishing classroom norms that discourage ridicule and promote mutual respect among peers can help students feel emotionally safe during oral activities. Moreover, confidence-building activities such as peer encouragement, self-reflection exercises, and positive reinforcement may help students view mistakes as opportunities for growth rather than personal shortcomings. These strategies may reduce fear of embarrassment and encourage more active participation in class.  

In relation to test anxiety, which was found to moderately affect students’ oral communication, the researchers recommend the use of Alternative and Flexible Assessment Methods. These may include performance-based assessments, oral portfolios, recorded presentations, and providing multiple opportunities for practice before graded tasks. Such approaches can help lessen the pressure associated with high-stakes oral examinations. Teachers may also conduct test-orientation sessions to familiarize students with assessment criteria and expectations. By clarifying procedures and offering sufficient practice opportunities, students’ fear of judgment and uncertainty during oral tests may be reduced.  

To lessen overall English class anxiety, the researchers recommend adopting student-centered and differentiated instructional strategies. Teachers should consider students’ varied learning styles and preferences when designing oral communication tasks. Scaffolded activities that address visual, auditory, and kinesthetic learning styles may help reduce anxiety and improve student participation. In addition, training students to use effective learning and coping strategies, such as self-monitoring, rehearsal techniques, and anxiety-management strategies, may empower them to handle oral communication tasks more confidently and effectively.  

Regarding the predictors of language anxiety, the findings revealed that self-confidence plays a significant role. Thus, the researchers propose the implementation of Systematic Confidence-Enhancement Programs. These may include regular speaking practices in supportive settings, recognition of students’ efforts, and goal-setting activities that allow learners to monitor their progress. Strengthening students’ self-confidence may encourage them to take risks in speaking and consequently reduce anxiety. 

Although language proficiency was not identified as the strongest predictor of anxiety, the findings suggest that emotional and social factors have a greater influence on students’ anxiety than linguistic competence alone. Therefore, the researchers recommend integrating communicative language practices rather than focusing solely on accuracy. Meaning-focused activities may help students feel less pressure to achieve grammatical perfection during oral communication tasks.  

To address anxiety resulting from peer influence, the researchers propose the use of Collaborative and Cooperative Learning strategies. Group activities that emphasize teamwork rather than competition may help reduce students’ fear of negative peer judgment. Establishing peer support systems and encouraging respectful communication among classmates can further contribute to a more inclusive and supportive learning environment.  

Finally, considering the influence of the classroom environment, particularly teacher support and competitive dynamics, the researchers recommend fostering a supportive and non-threatening classroom climate. Teachers should model empathy, provide consistent encouragement, and avoid overly competitive practices that may heighten students’ anxiety. A classroom environment that values effort, improvement, and mutual respect may significantly reduce students’ language anxiety in oral communication and promote more meaningful learning experiences.

Chapter 5 

SUMMARY, CONCLUSIONS, AND RECOMMENDATIONS 

 

This chapter presents the summary of the study, major findings, conclusions drawn, and recommendations proposed based on the analysis of predictors of language anxiety in oral communication among Grade 12 students of Mabini Colleges, Inc. 

 

Summary 

This study aimed to determine the predictors of language anxiety in oral communication among Grade 12 students of Mabini Colleges, Inc. Specifically, it sought to answer the following research questions: (1) What is the level of English language anxiety among the Grade 12 students in terms of communication anxiety, fear of negative evaluation, test anxiety, and English class anxiety; (2) What is the degree of existence of the predictors of language anxiety in terms of self-confidence, language proficiency, peer influence, and classroom environment; (3) What are the major predictors of language anxiety in oral communication among Grade 12 students; and (4) What interventions may be proposed to the institution to address language anxiety in oral communication. 

The study employed a quantitative research approach using a descriptive research design. This design was deemed appropriate in describing the level of English language anxiety, determining the degree of existence of its predictors, and identifying the major factors influencing oral communication anxiety among the respondents. The study focused on Grade 12 students of Mabini Colleges, Inc. as respondents, as they are at a stage where oral communication skills are critical for academic performance and future professional engagement.  

A researcher-made survey questionnaire was used as the primary instrument for data gathering. The questionnaire was structured to measure the level of English language anxiety in terms of communication anxiety, fear of negative evaluation, test anxiety, and English class anxiety, as well as the degree of existence of the predictors of language anxiety, namely self-confidence, language proficiency, peer influence, and classroom environment. Prior to its administration, the instrument was subjected to expert validation, pilot testing, and reliability testing using Cronbach’s alpha to ensure its validity and reliability. 

The data gathered were analyzed using appropriate descriptive statistical tools, including frequency and percentage to describe the profile of the respondents, and weighted mean to determine the level of English language anxiety and the degree of existence of its predictors. These statistical treatments enabled the researcher to systematically address each statement of the problem and provide a clear quantitative description of language anxiety in oral communication among Grade 12 students. 

Through this methodological approach, the study provided a comprehensive description of English language anxiety and its predictors, serving as a basis for identifying areas that may be addressed through institutional interventions aimed at improving students’ confidence and effectiveness in oral communication. 

 

Findings 

The following were the major findings of the study; 

1. Level of English Language Anxiety among Grade 12 Students  

The findings reveal that Grade 12 students of Mabini Colleges, Inc. experience a high level of English language anxiety across all dimensions measured. In terms of Communication Anxiety, the overall weighted mean was 2.92, interpreted as High. The highest-rated indicator obtained a weighted mean of 3.03 (High), indicating that certain oral communication situations strongly contribute to students’ anxiety. Meanwhile, the lowest-rated indicator registered a weighted mean of 2.76 (High), suggesting that although it contributes less compared to other indicators, it still affects students’ anxiety during oral communication.  

For Fear of Negative Evaluation, the overall weighted mean was 2.79, interpreted as High. The indicator “I feel embarrassed when I make a mistake while speaking English in class” obtained the highest weighted mean of 2.88 (High), and the remaining indicators yielded weighted means ranging from 2.74 to 2.85, also interpreted as High. This indicates that students commonly worry about making mistakes, pronunciation, and being judged negatively by others. With respect to Test Anxiety, the overall weighted mean was 2.25, verbally interpreted as Moderate. The highest weighted mean was recorded for the indicator “I panic when I am asked to speak in English during a test” at 2.36 (Moderate), while the lowest weighted mean was 2.15 (Moderate) for nervousness before English tests. These results indicate that students experience consistent but not extreme anxiety during English-related assessments involving oral communication. Regarding English Class Anxiety, the overall weighted mean was 2.38, interpreted as Moderate. The indicators under this variable yielded weighted means ranging from 2.22 to 2.51, interpreted as Moderate and High. The highest weighted mean (2.51) was obtained by the indicator related to nervousness when being called on in class, while the lowest (2.22) referred to feeling more tense in English class compared to other subjects.  

2. Degree of Existence of the Predictors of Language Anxiety In terms of Self-Confidence, the overall weighted mean was 2.52, interpreted as Agree. The indicators yielded weighted means ranging from 2.27 to 2.64, indicating that low self-confidence exists as a predictor of language anxiety. Students commonly reported feelings of embarrassment when making mistakes and doubt in their ability to express ideas clearly in English. For Language Proficiency, the overall weighted mean was 2.44, verbally interpreted as Disagree. The indicators under this variable obtained weighted means ranging from 2.37 to 2.56, suggesting that limited vocabulary, grammar difficulties, and lack of fluency exist but are not strongly perceived by most students as major contributors to their language anxiety. In terms of Peer Influence, the overall weighted mean was 2.50, interpreted as Agree. The indicators yielded weighted means ranging from 2.35 to 2.69. The highest weighted mean (2.69) corresponded to anxiety caused by classmates laughing at English mistakes, while indicators related to comparison with peers and pairing with proficient classmates obtained lower weighted means interpreted as Disagree. Regarding Classroom Environment, the overall weighted mean was 2.50, interpreted as Agree. The indicators under this variable yielded weighted means ranging from 2.21 to 2.97. The highest weighted mean (2.97) reflected reduced anxiety in supportive classroom environments, while indicators related to group activities and classroom atmosphere obtained lower weighted means interpreted as Disagree.  

3. Major Predictors of Language Anxiety in Oral Communication Based on the computed overall weighted means, self-confidence (2.52), peer influence (2.50), and classroom environment (2.50) were identified as the major predictors of language anxiety in oral communication, all interpreted as Agree. Among these, self-confidence registered the highest overall weighted mean, indicating that emotional and psychological factors play a more prominent role in students’ language anxiety than language proficiency (2.44, Disagree).  

4. Proposed Interventions to Address Language Anxiety The findings of the study served as the basis for identifying interventions aimed at addressing language anxiety in oral communication. These interventions focus on enhancing students’ self-confidence, encouraging positive peer interactions, and creating a supportive classroom environment to help reduce anxiety and improve students’ oral communication experiences in English. 

 

Conclusions 

The conclusions drawn from the findings of the study are as follows:  

1. Grade 12 students of Mabini Colleges, Inc. experience a high level of English language anxiety in oral communication in terms of communication anxiety and fear of negative evaluation, while test anxiety, and English class anxiety experience a moderate level. This implies that while students are able to participate in oral English activities, they still experience noticeable nervousness, discomfort, and apprehension in speaking situations, especially during class participation and evaluative tasks.  

2. The predictors of language anxiety—self-confidence, peer influence, and classroom environment—exist to a moderate degree, while language proficiency exists to a lesser extent. This implies that affective and social factors play a more substantial role than linguistic limitations influencing students’ anxiety during oral communication.  

3. Self-confidence emerged as the strongest predictor of language anxiety among Grade 12 students, followed by peer influence and classroom environment. This implies that students’ fear of making mistakes, embarrassment when speaking, and concern over peers’ reactions significantly contribute to heightened anxiety during oral English communication.  

4. Peer-related factors, particularly negative reactions such as laughter and pressure to perform well, contribute to students’ language anxiety, indicating that the social dynamics within the classroom influence students’ willingness and confidence to speak in English.  

5. The classroom environment moderately affects students’ language anxiety, suggesting that supportive teaching practices help reduce anxiety, while competitive classroom dynamics may heighten students’ nervousness during oral communication activities.  

6. The proposed interventions focusing on confidence-building activities, positive peer interaction, language support, and a supportive classroom environment may help reduce language anxiety in oral communication. This implies that structured and targeted interventions can create a more conducive learning atmosphere that encourages students to participate confidently and effectively in English oral communication. 

 

Recommendations 

Based on the conclusions of the study, the following recommendations are proposed;  

1. Teachers may implement regular confidence-building activities such as structured oral drills, role-playing, group discussions, and guided impromptu speaking tasks. These activities should gradually increase in difficulty to allow students to develop confidence and reduce fear when speaking in English.  

2. The school may encourage cooperative learning strategies that emphasize teamwork rather than competition. Clear classroom policies that discourage ridicule, teasing, or negative peer reactions should be reinforced to create a safe environment where students feel comfortable expressing themselves in English.  

3. English teachers may adopt learner-centered and supportive instructional strategies, such as providing constructive and non-threatening feedback, allowing sufficient preparation time before oral tasks, and acknowledging students’ efforts. These practices may help reduce communication anxiety and fear of negative evaluation.  

4. Although language proficiency was found to be a lesser predictor, the institution may still offer remedial English programs, pronunciation workshops, and language clinics to support students who struggle with basic language skills. Strengthening these skills may further lessen students’ anxiety during oral communication.  

5. Teachers may consider using varied and flexible assessment methods, such as group oral presentations, peer-assisted activities, and formative oral assessments, to minimize pressure during oral evaluations and reduce test-related anxiety.  

6. Mabini Colleges, Inc. may provide professional development programs for teachers focusing on managing language anxiety, fostering positive classroom dynamics, and implementing anxiety-reducing instructional strategies to improve students’ oral communication experiences.  

7. Future researchers may conduct similar studies involving other grade levels, institutions, or additional variables such as motivation and learning styles to further explore factors affecting language anxiety and to validate the findings of this study
